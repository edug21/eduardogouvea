Context

A national financial system made of independent cooperatives.

Sicredi is a cooperative financial institution: its members are also its owners, and each cooperative is run locally, close to the communities it serves. In 2018 the system had 116 cooperatives, more than 3.9 million members and over 1,600 branches across 22 states and the Federal District.

Rural credit sits at the heart of that business. For producers, it is the money that pays for seeds, inputs and services before planting. Every operation needs a Technical Rural Project, prepared by an agricultural technician and approved by the cooperative.

  1. 3.9M+MembersProducers and account holders who own the cooperatives
  2. 116CooperativesLocal, autonomous, each with its own way of working
  3. 5Central cooperativesRegional coordination and supervision
  4. 1Sicredi (CAS)Confederation and Cooperative Bank: shared services, products and risk

The challenge

A critical process was still being built manually.

Every Sicredi cooperative created its Technical Rural Credit Projects its own way, in isolated tools, many running outdated technology, with no data structure or reusable history.

The result: constant rework, zero visibility into project status, and delays releasing funds to rural producers who depended on this credit to plant their crops.

  • Manual creation, no standard across cooperatives
  • No centralized visibility into status
  • Constant operational rework
  • Data from previous projects lost
  • Delays releasing funds to producers
Where it came from

The project was born inside Sicredi's Rural Credit Evolution Program, which mapped 47 improvement opportunities and prioritized them collectively with business stakeholders.

The Technical Rural Project was one of the fronts chosen in that process.

Not a problem I decided to solve on my own.

The solution

A rural project broken into structured blocks, no longer one continuous form.

Protec Agro split the Technical Project into independent modules (associate data, budget, feasibility, environmental licensing, among others), reducing cognitive load and enabling data reuse across blocks.

Design decisions

Three decisions that sustained the product at scale.

01

Designing for data reuse, not just for the screen

Problem
Information already captured in previous projects was re-entered from scratch on every new project.
Decision
Create an Associate module that stores data entered by both the Cooperative and the Technician, reusable in the future.
Outcome
Less repeated data entry, less human error, and a structured database for future analysis.
02

Standardization without rigidity

Problem
Regional requirements varied across cooperatives: crops, financing ranges, technology.
Decision
Allow each Cooperative to configure project parameters while keeping a standard structure underneath.
Outcome
A single architecture sustaining hundreds of different regional configurations.
03

Access designed around responsibility

Problem
Four user profiles, four completely different information needs.
Decision
Structure access by role: CAS sees everything, the Cooperative sees its network, the Technician sees their associates, the Associate sees their own operation.
Outcome
Each profile sees exactly what it needs: less noise, less operational error at scale.

What testing changed

What we only discovered by testing with real users.

01The design didn't fit the user's screen

We designed the screens at 1920px wide. But cooperatives mostly worked on laptops with factory-default settings at 1366px, the most common resolution in Brazil. We found this out in the very first round of testing, and adapted the entire interface to that real resolution.

1920px→1366px

02Modals hid the information users needed

Users needed to reference data already shown on screen to complete or calculate a new entry. The modals we used hid exactly that reference. We replaced them with sliding side panels that keep context visible while the user enters new data.

Modal→Side panel

03The original training plan didn't scale

The initial request was extensive documentation: screenshots, detailed descriptions of every feature, and step-by-step instructions for every screen. After reviewing previous training materials, it became clear that producing and keeping this updated with every new release would be unfeasible for the team.

Planned

Extensive written documentation, screen by screen, with screenshots and detailed step-by-step instructions.

Didn't scale with the release pace
Adopted

Short, narrated videos, organized by product section, available on demand inside the system itself.

Learnings

Enterprise product design is rarely solved on the screen.

The real work was structuring how people, rules and data intersect: four actors, one shared process and a data model that outlives each project. That structure is why the platform scaled to new credit lines and fronts after launch, without being redesigned.

The other lesson came from testing early on real equipment. The screen size, the modals and the training plan all changed because of what real users showed us, not because of what we assumed.

Context

A hospital-wide record system, and one of its most demanding specialties.

Philips is a global health technology company. In Latin America, its flagship clinical software was Tasy, a hospital management platform and electronic medical record (EMR) created in Blumenau, Brazil, and used by more than 2,000 healthcare institutions across the region.

Tasy covers the whole hospital, from admission and billing to the clinical record. Oncology is one of its most complex modules, because cancer treatment is not a single event but a long, planned sequence where every step depends on the one before.

  1. 01Diagnosis & stagingType of cancer and how far it has spread. It defines everything that follows.
  2. 02ProtocolThe treatment plan: which drugs, in which doses, on which schedule.
  3. 03Cycles & sessionsTreatment repeats in cycles, each with planned days of administration.
  4. 04AdministrationPharmacy prepares and checks each dose; nursing administers and records it.

Oncologists, pharmacists and nurses all work on the same treatment, each from a different angle, and they need to see where the patient is in that sequence at any moment.

The problem

The complexity wasn't in the information. It was in the navigation.

Tasy already held every clinical data point needed: diagnosis, staging, protocol, medications, doses, cycles, sessions, authorizations. The problem was never a lack of data.

It was structural: every clinical context lived in its own panel, and every panel required opening a new system session to be consulted. In practice, a doctor or pharmacist trying to answer a simple question like “what's this patient's next cycle, and what's already been administered so far?” had to:

  • Open one session to check diagnosis and staging
  • Open another session to check the defined protocol
  • Open another to see cycles and status
  • Open another to check medications and doses for the current session

Every panel switch broke the context already built mentally. The professional carried information “in their head” from one screen to the next, in a domain where a context error has a direct consequence for the patient.

Legacy flow: panel fragmentation

Step through the original navigation. The numbers in each description point to the matching markers on the screen.

The real challenge
It wasn't showing more or less information. It was eliminating the fragmentation that forced professionals to rebuild the same clinical reasoning over and over, screen after screen.

My role

UX Lead heading a team of three designers.

My responsibility went beyond the final interface.

Discovery

Led and synthesized the conversations with the doctor, pharmacist and oncology specialist, translating clinical pain points into actionable product requirements for the team.

Alignment

Aligned with the PO and Business Analysts on which critical information couldn't be reduced for safety and traceability, versus what could be freely reorganized.

Team direction

Directed the other two designers in building the multi-level component, keeping the pattern consistent across the oncology flow's screens.

Feasibility

Worked with the Technical Consultant and Developer to defend the technical feasibility of the new navigation structure within the constraints of Tasy's legacy platform.

Discovery

Understanding the problem with the people who live the treatment.

To validate and deepen this hypothesis, I ran direct conversations with three key roles in the clinical journey.

Oncologist

Explained how decision-making depends on quickly comparing staging, protocol and cycle history, and how fragmented navigation slowed down that reasoning day to day.

Oncology specialist

Articulated the pain points of the current interface and brought competitor references, helping calibrate what the hospital-systems market was already solving better.

Pharmacist

Brought depth on how medications, doses and solutions need to be cross-checked safely before administration, reinforcing why this information couldn't be hidden, only reorganized.

These conversations confirmed the diagnosis: the problem wasn't the absence or excess of clinical data, but the navigation architecture that artificially separated it into sealed compartments.

The direction that came out of discovery

Instead of separate panels and sessions, the treatment would be read through three stacked levels of information.

  1. 1PatientWho they are, diagnosis, staging
  2. 2TreatmentProtocol, current cycle, status
  3. 3ProgressSessions, medications, history

Design decisions

From panel-based navigation to a layered view.

Getting there took several rounds of studies and prototypes. Many were refuted by the specialists along the way; the ones that survived kept every clinical detail and removed the navigation between them. Three decisions held:

Some of the explorations

01

From isolated panels to a multi-level component

The core decision of the redesign was to build a single component able to group up to three levels of information at once, on the same screen: patient context, treatment context, and progress context (sessions, medications, history). Instead of forcing the professional to open a new session for every layer, that information now coexists on one screen, with a clear visual hierarchy between what's essential and what's supporting detail.

Why this approach, and not others

Simplifying by removing fields was ruled out early: data like dose, medication and status is critical for safety and auditability. The alternative chosen was to keep the data depth intact, but eliminate the navigation cost between it.

02

Making treatment a journey, not a collection of screens

The experience was reorganized around the real treatment sequence, from diagnosis to administration, instead of mirroring the system's internal module structure.

This aligned the interface with the professional's mental model: they think in terms of the patient's treatment, not the software's features.

03

Progressive disclosure as an answer to data volume

To sustain the unified view without overloading the screen, information was organized into depth layers:

  1. Summary
  2. Detail
  3. History

This lets the professional access the level of granularity they need in the moment, without losing the rest of the context.

The solution

One continuous view instead of sessions opened in succession.

The same task, locating a patient's full treatment history, used to require multiple sessions opened in succession. Now everything lives in a single continuous view.

BeforeAfter

Before and after. Drag the handle to compare the protocols list with the new layered view.

Before

The flow from selecting the patient's treatment through to the medication stage within the cycles: an extensive navigation spread across numerous separate panels.

The experience was organized around individual functionalities:

  • Staging
  • Protocols
  • Medications
  • Cycles
  • Sessions
  • Administration
  • History

The healthcare professional had to connect these pieces of information themselves.

After

The experience is now organized around the treatment journey:

  1. Patient
  2. Diagnosis & Staging
  3. Treatment
  4. Protocol
  5. Current Cycle
  6. Session
  7. Medication
  8. Administration

The interface now tells the story of the treatment.

Outcome

One visual context instead of rebuilding reasoning at every screen.

In usability tests, the specialists who took part searched for a patient's treatment information through the new flow. Counting the steps of each path, the same task took a third of the steps of the legacy navigation: one continuous view instead of four sessions opened in succession.

Their feedback after the validations and tests was that the structure finally matched day-to-day clinical reasoning: they stopped rebuilding context at every screen change.

Status

The solution was validated as a concept and was not implemented. The step reduction comes from usability testing, not from production data.

Learnings

In clinical systems, the design work is in the relationships between information, not in isolated screens.

In a clinical system, a single piece of information rarely has meaning on its own:

  1. Diagnosis influences staging
  2. Staging influences treatment
  3. Treatment defines the protocol
  4. The protocol defines medications
  5. Medications are organized into cycles and sessions

Showing less was never the answer. The job was to make that chain visible and navigable in one place, turning a collection of records, protocols, medications, cycles and sessions into a coherent picture of the patient's treatment.

The goal was simple: make treatment visible.

Next steps

Where this approach could go next.

Building on this approach, a few opportunities could evolve in future iterations:

  • Treatment timelineExpand the temporal representation to cover the patient's entire journey, not just the current cycle.
  • Personalized viewsAdapt the information density to the professional's role: physician, nursing, pharmacy or administration.
  • Clinical decision supportExplore how to surface relevant clinical information at the exact moment of decision, without increasing cognitive load.
  • Integrated dashboardConsolidate diagnosis, staging, protocol, cycles, sessions and administration into a single tracking view.
  • Smarter notificationsMake changes, pending items and next steps more visible without creating an excessive number of alerts.

Context

A close runoff, and a flood of information that is hard to compare.

Brazil's 2026 presidential election went to a second round between Lula (PT) and Flávio Bolsonaro (PL), separated by less than two percentage points in the first round.

Voters had no shortage of content: government plans, interviews, debates, polls, court cases. What they lacked was a way to put the two candidates on the same footing. Each source covered one side, one topic or one moment, and most of it arrived already framed by someone's opinion.

This was a personal project, with no ties to any campaign or party. The goal was to make each candidate's information easier to reach for anyone still deciding.

The challenge

Comparable, verifiable and neutral, in a format people can actually read.

The site had to hold a large and sensitive body of information without taking a side, and stay readable for someone deciding their vote on a phone.

  • Promises, approved laws and opinions mixed together in coverage
  • Investigations reported as if they were convictions, and the reverse
  • Different depth of coverage for each candidate
  • Facts changing every few days: polls, endorsements, debates, votes in Congress
  • Dense legal and economic language that excludes most readers

Principles

Three rules that shaped every screen.

Same structure for both

Same themes, same order, same labels and the same visual weight. Neither candidate ever appears first in a way the other doesn't.

Every fact has a source

Each statement carries a small number that opens its source. Opinions are attributed to whoever said them, never stated by the page.

Say what a fact is

A promise is not a law, an investigation is not a conviction, and an annulled case is not an acquittal. The page names the difference every time.

Design decisions

Turning editorial rules into interface.

01

Mirrored pairs instead of separate profiles

Every topic is a row with two cells, one per candidate, always in the same position and color. A sticky header keeps both names visible while reading, so the comparison happens at a glance instead of in the reader's memory.

02

Labels that separate fact from promise

A small set of labels tells the reader what kind of information they are looking at: in the registered plan, said in public, depends on Congress, approved law, under investigation, procedural decision. The labels are explained once, before the content, and reused everywhere.

03

Who gains, who pays

For the proposals with the biggest impact, the first thing shown is who benefits and who bears the cost, as plain chips. Below come the legal path, the arguments for and against, and their sources. Readers get the consequence first and the detail on demand.

This came from a visitor. She suggested highlighting, for both candidates, whenever companies or the population were the ones who benefit. It shows the real impact on people, and applied to both sides it doesn't break neutrality.

04

A fixed format for investigations

Every case, for both candidates, follows the same template: a four-step bar showing the most serious stage reached, then what it is, what is proven, what changed and whether guilt was recognized by the courts. The format prevents tone from doing the judging.

05

A one-minute summary in plain language

A full-width band, in inverted contrast, answers the question most people actually have: what could change if each one wins. It is written for anyone to understand, and it still keeps the source numbers and the views of supporters and critics side by side.

The solution

Built for the phone, where people decide.

Most of this content is read and shared on a phone. On small screens the pairs stack, each cell names its candidate, the section menu scrolls sideways, and a share button sends a link to any section through WhatsApp, Telegram and other networks.

Reception

Shared hand to hand, read on the phone, and checked by its own readers.

There was no budget and no paid promotion, only my own posts. Visits peaked at 325 on launch day and kept coming until the runoff, with 144 shares through the site's own button. Most arrived with no referrer, the usual trace of links passed along in messaging apps; among the identified sources, Instagram led, followed by LinkedIn.

Read where people decide

72% of visits came from phones and 85% from Brazil. The mobile layout was not an adaptation, it was the main product.

Audited by a reader

A visitor sent an independent analysis of accuracy, sources and impartiality. It confirmed the key facts against official sources and found no intentional bias toward either side.

Useful to the undecided

Unprompted messages described it as something unique and essential for people still on the fence, exactly the audience it was built for.

The same analysis also pointed out subtle asymmetries: more items and a warmer tone for one candidate in the plain-language summary, and a longer list of investigations for the other. Not distortions, but a reminder that a balanced structure doesn't guarantee a balanced tone.

Iteration

A living document, corrected in public.

The campaign kept moving, so the site did too, and readers became part of the process. Each update is logged in a public "What changed" list, with the date and what was added or corrected.

  • First version from a comparative report, then expanded with Congress, endorsements, polls and the legal basis of each proposal
  • A source audit checked 345 statements and corrected details in four investigation cases
  • "Who gains / who pays" brought to the top of each high-impact proposal, after a visitor's suggestion, to show the real impact on people first
  • Polls, debates, endorsements and a constitutional amendment added as they happened, up to two days before the runoff

Learnings

In civic information, neutrality is a design decision, not a tone of voice.

Fairness here came from structure more than from wording: the same slots for both sides, the same labels, the same template for every case. Once the rules were part of the interface, they held even when the content was difficult.

The second lesson was about trust. Showing the source of every line, and logging every correction, did more for credibility than any claim of being impartial.

The third was that neutrality has to be checked from the outside. I was careful with structure, yet a reader still found tone differences I hadn't seen. Opening the work to review is part of the design.

The goal was simple:
Help people compare, not tell them what to choose.

PowerFit · Design challenge

An onboarding that collects seven data points with almost no typing.

A five-day challenge from a recruitment process: the first-login onboarding for PowerFit’s weight-loss app, shaped by 12 interviews across three user profiles and validated with real participants.

Context

A supplement brand launching its own weight-loss app.

PowerFit® is one of the best-known sports nutrition brands in the United States, with supplements for building muscle, losing weight and improving performance. It was launching a weight-loss mobile app alongside one of its popular supplements.

DeepX, the agency in charge of the app for Android and iOS, used this brief as a design challenge in its recruitment process. The company recognized and valued the result.

The challenge

Seven inputs on first login, from a brief with few details.

The app asks for program information the first time a user logs in. The brief listed what to collect, but said little about interactions or business rules, so assumption mapping had to come before any screen.

  1. Unit system
  2. Gender
  3. Height
  4. Weight
  5. Age
  6. Body type
  7. Activity level
  • Body type with three options: ectomorph, endomorph and mesomorph
  • Activity level: sedentary, moderately active or extremely active
  • Metric or imperial units, chosen before any measurement
  • A basic wireframe from the brief, with little about components or behavior

Discovery

Learning the patterns of the category, then naming what I didn’t know.

I went through the full experience of reference apps, from onboarding and account creation to updating personal data later, to find the patterns people already expect in fitness and health apps.

Users

Three profiles, twelve conversations.

Talking to my personal trainer, health professionals and gym staff narrowed the audience to three profiles:

  • Athletes and professionals
  • Gym-goers, young adults
  • Gym-goers, older adults

I then interviewed four people from each one, two men and two women.

Athletes and professionals

  • Train often, with strict workout and diet routines
  • Rely on professional services for guidance
  • Use supplements regularly
  • Prefer advanced, professional-grade tools

Gym-goers, young adults

  • Exercise mainly for enjoyment, and regularly
  • Skip sessions without feeling guilty
  • Use supplements for performance or on medical advice
  • Try many apps, often ones with gamification

Gym-goers, older adults

  • Work out mainly for health, and regularly
  • Skip sessions without feeling guilty
  • Use supplements mostly on medical advice
  • Rarely use training apps; prefer a professional

The solution

From whiteboard sketches to a prototype tested with users.

I think by sketching. I mapped the main flows by hand, the way I usually do with the team on a whiteboard, so ideas can be debated before they are drawn properly.

Wireframes then tested structure, readability, hierarchy and balance: one question per screen, choices made by tapping instead of typing.

To validate, I brought back participants from the interviews, gave the prototype a context drawn from their own answers and watched them use it, collecting feedback as they moved through the flow.

Learnings

Five days is enough to design, not enough to iterate.

I adapted the process I use in real products to a five-day deadline. The proposal only started to flow after feedback from all three profiles, and testing began later than I wanted.

I missed debating ideas with a multidisciplinary team that knew the context. Collaborative sketching with technical and business people is always where my best ideas get tested first. With more time, the next step would be another round of usability tests and iterations on the proposed flow.

The goal was simple:
ask for less, so people finish.

Promove+ · Design challenge

Making progress visible and peers closer on a social learning platform.

A two-week challenge from a recruitment process: redesigning the course page of PROMOVE, a platform where learners exchange experiences with peers and trainers. Discovery with users and competitors came first, then two interface models validated with real learners.

Context

A learning platform built around learning from each other.

PROMOVE is a learning platform focused on the social side of learning: learners exchange experiences with their peers and trainers. The page in the brief was the home of a learner going through an onboarding program with new colleagues and managers.

It was a recruitment challenge, and the result exceeded my own expectations and was very well received by the company. I split the two weeks in two moments: understanding the problem with a CSD matrix and conversations with users, then designing, prototyping and validating with some of those same users.

The challenge

Show learners their progress, and get them talking to each other.

The brief asked for a redesign of the learner’s main page, to make progress in the program clear and motivate engagement with other people. It was short on interactions and business rules, and the wireframe that came with it was basic. It had four blocks:

  • My progress: every task in the program, with a bar showing where the learner is
  • Your learning journey: the full list of tasks
  • Feed: the latest messages exchanged by everyone in the program
  • Groups: the learners in each group and their individual progress

The deliverable: a high-fidelity, responsive wireframe, with a short note on the process.

Discovery

Sorting what I knew, what I assumed and what I had to ask.

A CSD matrix organized the brief into certainties, suppositions to validate and open doubts. The doubts became the script for the user conversations.

C

Certainties

  • Focus on the course stream, not on user profiles.
  • Focus on the information architecture of the screens.
  • A simple, clean flow.
  • The user is already logged in on this screen.
S

Suppositions

  • Learners arrive from a profile page with their courses.
  • “My learning journey” links to the lessons.
  • The journey shows the same progress as the progress bar.
  • After a lesson, learners do exercises and talk to peers.
  • The peer group is visible on the course page.
  • Groups use the same progress bar as the learner.
D

Doubts

  • What matters most on a course page?
  • What is the routine of distance learners?
  • Is seeing course progress important?
  • Exercises on one screen, or split by lesson?
  • Several groups per course, or one?
  • Should related courses be reachable from the page?
  • Should the introduction text always be visible?
  • Can lessons be watched out of order?
  • A quiz after each lesson?

The solution

From a sitemap to three ways of reading a course.

With the brief, the original wireframe, the interviews and the benchmark in hand, I drafted the site structure. It showed how learners would reach the course page and what deserved the most emphasis once there.

A new wireframe changed much of the original and went through several rounds with selected learners, refined after each one.

From the wireframe elements came a style guide: a refined color palette and typography, and the core UI components used across every screen.

The prototypes explored two ways to organize the course experience.

One-page overview

Every section of the course in one scrollable page: title, details, description, lessons grouped by stage, instructors and the learners enrolled. A lesson opens its own page with progress, the lesson list, content, exercises and a feed.

Chat

A real-time chat beside the lesson, so learners can talk to classmates watching the same content. Designed for the full height and width of the screen.

The whole design is responsive. The screens below show how the course and lesson pages behave on a phone.

Learnings

Strong opinions from users made up for what the brief left out.

I really enjoyed the challenge. Working closer to the technical team, stakeholders and real users would have clarified the business rules and made some choices more assertive.

Still, discovery and understanding grew a lot along the way, built on clear, strong opinions from many different learners.

The goal was simple:
show where you are, and who is learning with you.
